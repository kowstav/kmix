Yes. In this setting, your idea is better understood as **reweighting the learning problem around residual mistakes** rather than simply “adding more text.”

If a word currently costs the final coder

\[
c_t=-\log_2 q_t(y_t)
\]

bits, and many expensive occurrences share a recognizable composition \(z_t\), then we can deliberately train the model much harder on that pattern. The decoder never needs to see the synthetic examples; it only receives the resulting model parameters. That is valid as long as those parameters are counted in the package.

The first thing I would test, though, is whether the pattern actually contains enough information. Suppose all occurrences matching some composition \(z\) have target-word distribution

\[
P(Y\mid z).
\]

Its irreducible uncertainty is

\[
\boxed{H(Y\mid z)=-\sum_yP(y\mid z)\log_2P(y\mid z)}.
\]

If \(H(Y\mid z)\) is small but the current model still pays many bits, that is exactly the kind of mistake synthetic training might fix.

For example, imagine the model repeatedly encounters a pattern equivalent to

\[
\text{there }\_\text{ a NOUN}
\]

and in the real corpus the missing word is distributed approximately

\[
P(\text{is})=0.70,\qquad
P(\text{was})=0.25,\qquad
P(\text{exists})=0.03,\ldots
\]

Then the pattern itself is genuinely predictive. If the current model still gives `is` only 0.1 probability, we have a training/model-capacity failure rather than unavoidable entropy.

Your augmentation idea would then make sense.

### But literally duplicating sentences is equivalent to changing their loss weight

If example \(t\) is repeated \(k_t\) times during training, then

\[
\sum_t k_t[-\log P_\theta(y_t\mid h_t)]
\]

is basically weighted cross-entropy.

So rather than physically creating thousands of copies, we can define

\[
\boxed{
L(\theta)
=
\sum_t w_t
[-\log P_\theta(y_t\mid h_t)]
}
\]

where expensive residual events receive larger \(w_t\).

Round 8 already points toward this issue: when expensive events are sampled disproportionately, the report says inverse sampling weights are needed if we want to preserve the original corpus objective. research_round8_report

But I think we can deliberately *depart* from the ordinary objective during a curriculum stage.

For example:

\[
w_t
=
1+\alpha\,g(c_t)
\]

where \(c_t\) is the current final-coder cost and \(g\) clips extreme values:

\[
g(c)=\min(c,C_{\max}).
\]

Then train the model to spend more capacity where the archive is actually spending bits.

After that, do a final phase on the true unweighted corpus objective so we don't destroy performance elsewhere.

## Your synthetic-pattern idea can do something weighting alone cannot

Suppose the original corpus contains:

> The compound undergoes rapid **decomposition** under heat.

and that `decomposition` is expensive.

If we merely replay the exact sentence, the model may memorize that sentence.

Instead extract a structural composition such as

\[
[\text{ENTITY}]
\quad
[\text{undergoes}]
\quad
[\text{ADJ}]
\quad
Y
\quad
[\text{under CONDITION}]
\]

and generate variations:

\[
A\text{ undergoes rapid }Y\text{ under }B
\]

\[
C\text{ undergoes thermal }Y\text{ under }D
\]

etc.

Now the model is forced to associate the **composition** with the target rather than memorizing one byte sequence.

Conceptually,

\[
\text{one observed hard event}
\rightarrow
\text{many training constraints sharing the same latent rule}.
\]

That could be useful.

But every synthetic transformation needs to preserve the information available at decoding time.

If the deployed model is causal,

\[
P(y_t\mid x_{<t}),
\]

we cannot train it to rely on a synthetic feature extracted from \(x_{>t}\) unless our codec explicitly decodes that future endpoint first, as in the constructor/content scheme.

---

## I would use positives *and negatives*

This is important for our lexical-echo work.

Suppose we discover:

\[
\text{no}\rightarrow\text{know}
\]

is sometimes useful.

We should not generate only training examples where `no` eventually leads to `know`.

Otherwise the model learns:

\[
P(\text{know}\mid\text{no})\uparrow
\]

everywhere, stealing probability when `know` is inappropriate.

Instead train pairs.

Positive:

\[
(\text{no},H)\rightarrow\text{know}
\]

Negative:

\[
(\text{no},H')\not\rightarrow\text{know}.
\]

Then the model learns the *gate*:

\[
G_\theta(\text{no},\text{know},H)
\]

rather than a global association.

This directly addresses Round 8's main problem: there **is** measurable semantic/directed association signal, but broad neighbor excitation creates too many false activations and doesn't pay for its representation. The directed next-content association did outperform shuffled and exact-echo controls, but only weakly. research_round8_report

# A more rigorous synthetic-training scheme

For every word occurrence \(t\), first compute its existing final-coder surprise:

\[
s_t=-\log_2P_0(y_t\mid H_t).
\]

Then identify a causal structural representation

\[
z_t=\phi(H_t).
\]

This could contain:

- previous constructor pattern,
- previous content words,
- article state,
- lexical echo activations,
- prefix/suffix/morphological relationships,
- punctuation state,
- distance since related words.

Now cluster high-cost events by \(z_t\).

For cluster \(C\),

\[
B_C=\sum_{t\in C}s_t
\]

is its total bit budget.

Then calculate empirical target entropy

\[
H_C=H(Y\mid C).
\]

The most interesting clusters are:

\[
\boxed{
B_C\text{ large}
\quad\land\quad
H_C\text{ small}.
}
\]

Those are regions where:

> the archive is spending lots of bits even though a reusable pattern should make the answer fairly predictable.

Those are the ones worth synthetically amplifying.

Not necessarily the spectacular 70-bit rare surname.

Round 7 explicitly warns about this: rare difficult words look dramatic but can have tiny aggregate cost; common words dominate total bit expenditure. research_round7_report

---

## Then generate a pattern family

For hard cluster \(C\), create transformations

\[
T_1(C),T_2(C),\ldots,T_K(C)
\]

that preserve the useful relation but vary irrelevant details.

The training objective might be

\[
L=
L_{\text{real}}
+
\lambda
L_{\text{synthetic}}
+
\beta R_{\text{weights}}.
\]

Where

\[
L_{\text{synthetic}}
=
-\sum_{C}
\sum_k
\log P_\theta
\left(
y_C\mid T_k(H_C)
\right).
\]

The synthetic term teaches invariance.

The real term prevents drifting away from the actual file.

And

\[
R_{\text{weights}}
\]

ensures that “learning more” isn't accomplished by storing a huge lookup table.

---

## We could make it even more compression-specific

Instead of asking:

> Which examples does the transformer find hard?

ask:

> Where does the **final cmix+transformer coder** still lose bits?

That distinction matters because previous rounds repeatedly found improvements to neural loss that disappeared after cmix.

So define residual regret for a candidate module:

\[
r_t
=
-\log_2P_{\rm final}(y_t)
+
\log_2P_{\rm candidate}(y_t).
\]

We want synthetic training to maximize

\[
\sum_t r_t
\]

on the real corpus, **after the final mixer**.

Round 8 explicitly proposes training a small residual directly against native final-bit logits rather than relying only on neural perplexity. research_round8_report

That is probably where I would start.

# There is also a particularly interesting variant: “error replay”

Imagine the compressor makes a high-cost prediction at position \(t\):

\[
P(\text{know})=0.00098
\]

while the actual word is `know`.

Instead of just taking one SGD update, put that event into a replay bank:

\[
M=\{(H_t,y_t,s_t)\}.
\]

Sampling probability could be

\[
P(t\in M)
\propto
s_t^\gamma.
\]

But the bank should group related events, so we repeatedly train on **families of mistakes**, not isolated positions.

Something like

\[
\text{priority}(C)
=
\frac{
\text{total final bits in cluster}
\times
\text{estimated learnability}
}{
\text{model capacity required}
}.
\]

That is almost exactly a compression-aware version of prioritized experience replay.

---

# And we can combine this with your constructor idea

Suppose content-first decoding gives us

\[
L\quad\_\quad R
\]

and the true constructor gap is \(G\).

If that gap was expensive, generate training variations preserving the endpoints/classes:

\[
(L_i,R_i,G)
\]

or related grammatical realizations.

Then train

\[
P(G\mid L,R,z)
\]

aggressively.

Likewise for CLEF:

\[
u\rightarrow v
\]

hard relations can receive synthetic replay with context perturbations.

So we could have a single training curriculum based on three kinds of residual mistake:

\[
\boxed{
\text{hard content selection}
+
\text{hard lexical associations}
+
\text{hard constructor gaps}.
}
\]

---

The one thing I would **not** do is simply make every rare or expensive word occur 100 times in synthetic text.

That can make the model assign too much global probability to those words and increase archive size elsewhere.

The target should be:

\[
\boxed{
\text{teach the model the condition under which the hard word becomes predictable}
}
\]

rather than:

\[
\text{teach the model that the hard word is common}.
\]

And that gives us a concrete experiment: take the existing final-bit traces, find the highest **aggregate-cost recurring context families**, measure \(H(Y\mid\phi(H))\), and identify cases where the current coder pays much more than that conditional entropy. Those are the strongest candidates for synthetic-pattern training.
