Yes. There is a potentially serious compression architecture in this idea, but I would change one important part: **don't think of it as a model guessing until it gets the article right. Think of it as encoding the article through progressively stronger constraints.**

That distinction makes it compatible with lossless compression.

The current Hutter record is 100,424,672 bytes, and the winning system is already approximately at the contest's ~50-hour, ~10-GB limits, so a multi-pass architecture probably has to **replace** substantial parts of the existing transformer rather than simply run alongside it. [Hutter1](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/) The current submission itself replaces fx2-cmix's LSTM with a transformer and uses PPMD outputs as transformer inputs. [GitHub](https://github.com/astOwOlfo/fx2-cmix-transformer-v1)

### What a "Wordle compressor" would actually encode

Suppose the real word is:

`compression`

and both compressor and decompressor deterministically predict:

`expression`

The compressor knows the answer and could calculate something like:

```text
prediction: e x p r e s s i o n
target:     c o m p r e s s i o n

feedback:   ...
```

The decompressor can produce exactly the same initial prediction.

What it **cannot** produce by itself is the Wordle feedback, because the feedback depends on the unknown true word.

So the archive has to contain the feedback.

That gives us a codec:

\[
\text{model guess}
\rightarrow
\text{encoded feedback}
\rightarrow
\text{new guess}
\rightarrow
\text{encoded feedback}
\rightarrow \cdots
\]

Eventually the target is uniquely determined.

That's completely valid lossless compression.

Mathematically, you could represent an article \(X\) as:

\[
X \leftrightarrow
(S,F_1,F_2,\ldots,F_k,R)
\]

where:

- \(S\) = structural information such as word lengths,
- \(F_i\) = refinement feedback for round \(i\),
- \(R\) = unresolved residual characters.

Then compress those streams with arithmetic coding:

\[
L =
-\log P(S)
-\sum_i \log P(F_i\mid S,F_{<i})
-\log P(R\mid S,F_{1:k})
\]

If the right side is smaller than ordinary next-byte coding, we've won.

## But random first guesses are probably a bad idea

Your first guess should not be random.

Suppose the true word is:

`electromagnetic`

and we randomly propose:

`qjwarpmzlxkfghi`

Nearly every feedback symbol will mean "wrong."

We've learned almost nothing efficiently.

Instead, the first proposal should be the model's **best deterministic reconstruction given the information already available**.

For example:

```text
Known:

Article topic: Maxwell equations
Sentence:
"The _____ field is generated..."

Word length: 14

Model guess:
electrostatic

Target:
electromagnetic
```

Now the difference between prediction and truth potentially contains far less information than the original word.

This becomes **learned predictive coding**.

And that is much more interesting.

# Your word-length model is especially useful

I would actually keep this part.

Instead of representing Wikipedia as a raw byte sequence:

```text
The electromagnetic field ...
```

first represent it approximately as:

```text
WORD(3)
SPACE
WORD(15)
SPACE
WORD(5)
SPACE
...
```

plus punctuation, XML structure, capitalization, digits, etc.

So Model A predicts/compresses:

\[
\text{structure}
\]

and Model B predicts:

\[
P(\text{letters}\mid \text{structure})
\]

Knowing that the next token is:

```text
lowercase word
length = 15
```

is valuable information.

A bidirectional model now knows there are exactly 15 positions it must fill.

This is fundamentally different from ordinary next-byte prediction.

### You don't necessarily need two complete models

I'd probably use one small shared network with different heads:

```text
                 shared representation
                         │
              ┌──────────┴──────────┐
              │                     │
        structure head         character head
              │                     │
       word lengths etc.       masked characters
```

That avoids paying for two independent sets of weights.

# Here's where your architecture could beat autoregression

A normal next-token compressor factorizes text like:

\[
P(X)=
P(x_1)
P(x_2|x_1)
P(x_3|x_1,x_2)
\cdots
\]

The fundamental weakness is obvious.

When predicting character 500, the model cannot use character 501.

Your approach could.

Imagine:

```text
The scientist was born in ____ and died in London.
```

An autoregressive model predicting the blank can't use:

`died in London`

if that occurs later.

But an iterative masked model can see the entire **structure of the article**, including information encoded in later regions.

After some positions are resolved:

```text
The scientist was born in _e__i_ and died in London.
```

the remaining prediction becomes much easier.

That's the genuinely interesting part of your proposal.

Not Wordle specifically.

It's **bidirectional progressive decoding**.

## A better feedback alphabet than literal Wordle

I don't think this:

\[
0,\;0.5,\;1
\]

is necessarily optimal.

Wordle's yellow state—

> character exists somewhere else

—is useful for humans because we're good at solving words.

A neural compressor may benefit from something more information-efficient.

For example:

```text
0 = prediction wrong
1 = prediction correct
```

Then lock every correct position.

After one pass:

```text
Prediction:
electrostatic

Truth:
electromagnet

State:
ele_tro_____t_
```

Run the model again conditioned on the confirmed characters.

Or use richer feedback only when profitable:

```text
00 = correct
01 = wrong character, correct character class
10 = character occurs elsewhere
11 = unrestricted correction
```

But every additional feedback state has a bit cost.

We would discover the best representation empirically.

# There may be an even better approach: reveal characters by confidence

Instead of predicting every position each pass, let the model calculate:

\[
P(x_i\mid\text{currently known information})
\]

for every unresolved position.

Suppose a word is:

```text
_ l e c t r o m a g n e t i _
```

and its confidence is:

```text
position     predicted    probability
1            e            .41
2            l            .999
3            e            .998
4            c            .997
...
15           c            .55
```

The codec can reveal the **easiest positions first**.

Critically, compressor and decompressor calculate the same confidence scores, so you don't have to encode which position comes next.

They deterministically choose:

\[
i^*=\arg\max_i \max_c P(c_i=c)
\]

Then arithmetic-code that character.

Now another position becomes easier.

Repeat.

This gives us:

### Confidence-ordered bidirectional coding

Instead of:

```text
left → right → left → right
```

we encode:

```text
easiest unknown symbol
↓
update context
↓
next easiest
↓
update context
...
```

That is, in my view, considerably cleaner than literal Wordle feedback.

And it captures exactly the property you're after.

# We can still retain Wordle-style whole-word proposals

Consider:

```text
Structure:
WORD(length=12)

Context:
"The process of ______ converts..."

Model candidate list:

photosynthesis    0.72
fermentation      0.09
transcription     0.06
...
```

Instead of predicting individual characters, we can initially predict:

`photosynthesis`

If correct, that's enormously cheap.

If it's wrong, don't transmit the entire correct word immediately.

Encode some constraint.

For example:

```text
length = 12
candidate rejected
prefix = "trans"
```

Then recompute:

```text
transcription    .71
transformation   .19
transportation   .04
...
```

And repeat.

That's conceptually your Wordle algorithm.

# Another version might work even better: candidate rank coding

Suppose the model predicts:

```text
1  photosynthesis
2  respiration
3  fermentation
4  transcription
5  oxidation
...
```

If the true word is number 4, encode simply:

\[
\text{rank}=4
\]

using a probability distribution over ranks.

For weird names:

```text
Schwarzschild
Chandrasekhar
Huygens
Riemann
```

the model might use morphology or retrieval to propose a candidate list.

If the word isn't present:

```text
ESCAPE
```

and fall back to character refinement.

That might be substantially more computationally efficient than several Wordle rounds.

So I'd envision:

```text
              word structure
                    ↓
         whole-word prediction
                    ↓
        ┌───────────┴────────────┐
        │                        │
 candidate found             candidate absent
        │                        │
     code rank           iterative character model
                                 │
                          residual byte coder
```

# The article-level aspect is possible too

I wouldn't ask the model to generate an entire article character-by-character on every round. That would almost certainly destroy the runtime budget.

Instead divide each article into blocks, perhaps:

\[
128-1024 \text{ bytes}
\]

or word blocks.

The hierarchy might be:

```text
ARTICLE
│
├── structural skeleton
│    ├── paragraphs
│    ├── sentences
│    ├── word lengths
│    ├── punctuation
│    └── markup
│
├── semantic/article state
│
├── block 1 proposal
│
├── block 1 refinement
│
├── block 2 proposal
│
├── block 2 refinement
│
└── exact residual stream
```

Now the model can maintain an article-level recurrent state without performing full quadratic attention over the whole article.

# A knowledge base could help, but there's a Hutter catch

Suppose an article contains:

```text
Albert Einstein
general relativity
Ulm
1879
Princeton
```

A knowledge model could realize those values belong together.

That's exactly the sort of thing that should improve compression.

But if we ship a 500 MB vector database with the compressor, we've lost immediately.

Everything necessary to reconstruct enwik9 has to be contained in the submission; the official rules require the self-extracting archive to reproduce enwik9 without external input. [Hutter1](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/)

So the interesting options are:

**Tiny learned knowledge representations**, where facts are compressed into model weights.

Or even better:

**dynamic retrieval from already decoded Wikipedia**.

Imagine we're decoding article number 50,000.

Articles 1–49,999 have already been reconstructed exactly.

We can build a tiny search/index structure in RAM from them.

Then the current article can retrieve:

```text
similar article
related name
previous phrase
matching infobox
```

This knowledge costs **zero archive bytes**, because both encoder and decoder construct it deterministically from text they already possess.

It costs RAM and computation, though.

This is a very attractive Hutter-specific idea.

---

# Vector retrieval becomes especially interesting with your architecture

Imagine the current article starts:

```text
<title>James Clerk Maxwell</title>
```

The decoder already knows that because we encoded it first.

A small embedding system searches previously decoded text and finds:

```text
electromagnetism
Michael Faraday
Maxwell equations
physics
Scotland
```

Then the iterative model gets these as context before reconstructing the rest.

So we get something resembling an LLM's:

- retrieval,
- long-term memory,
- article context,
- attention,

without shipping an enormous database.

# One fundamental limit still applies

There is no information-theoretic free lunch from changing the communication format.

If the Wordle feedback uniquely determines the article, then ultimately:

\[
H(\text{article})
=
H(\text{complete feedback sequence})
\]

under an ideal representation.

So merely replacing characters with green/yellow/gray signals doesn't inherently compress anything.

The opportunity comes because a **finite 6M-parameter model may model the feedback/refinement process much better than it models left-to-right bytes**.

That is the actual research hypothesis.

We aren't trying to defeat information theory.

We're trying to find a better factorization that a tiny CPU-friendly model can approximate.

## Where I'd modify your original idea

Your version:

```text
predict word lengths
      ↓
generate arbitrary article
      ↓
Wordle feedback
      ↓
generate again
      ↓
feedback
      ↓
...
```

I'd make it:

```text
reversible Wikipedia parser
          ↓
encode structural skeleton
(word lengths / markup / punctuation)
          ↓
small bidirectional model
          ↓
deterministic best proposal
          ↓
lock very-high-confidence positions
          ↓
arithmetic-code constraints/corrections
          ↓
rerun only unresolved positions
          ↓
2–4 refinement rounds
          ↓
current cmix/PPMD system handles residual
```

And potentially:

```text
retrieval from previously decoded articles
                    ↓
             model context
```

This would be **lossless, deterministic and actually benchmarkable**.

### One more potentially important change

I would train the model specifically for:

\[
\boxed{\text{minimum final compressed size}}
\]

rather than merely next-token cross entropy.

For example, during training randomly mask portions of a word/article:

```text
el__tro_agnetism
```

and train the network to minimize the entropy of the remaining unknown characters.

Then:

```text
e___tro_agnet_sm
```

then:

```text
electro____tism
```

etc.

So training mimics decompression.

The network learns:

> Given an arbitrary partially decoded article, what missing information should become easiest next?

That's substantially different from ordinary language-model training.

I'd call the architecture something like a **Progressive Constraint Coder** rather than a Wordle model.

And I think **this is a more promising direction than the OCR idea**. The OCR approach added a new representation but little new information; this approach changes the *conditioning structure itself* and gives a small model access to right-context and globally revealed constraints—something the current bytewise next-token transformer cannot naturally exploit in the same way.
